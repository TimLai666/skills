# Story, script, shot list, storyboard test

Read at stages 1 to 3.

## Find the story

1. Write ten one-line versions of the user's idea, varying who, when, scale and form. Drop the first three: they are the ones anyone would write. Offer the user two or three of the rest, your pick first with a reason. Invent a story yourself only if they ask.
2. Test the one they pick in five lines: the situation, what someone wants, what stands in the way, what changes, what is left.
3. Write the ending first. Then make sure everything the ending needs (an object, a rule, who someone is) is in the first third.
4. Check that the story rises and falls. For each stretch, name what the tension does. A film where two people wait is flat however good the idea: someone has to act against someone, things have to get worse before the turn, and the high point has to be something seen, not only said.

## Write it

- Show, do not tell. A line never describes what the picture shows; it adds what the picture cannot.
- Few words. One idea per line. The moral is never spoken.
- Every character who matters does something. A character who only watches is a prop.
- Give each important beat its own sound moment and its own stillness around it.
- Decide where the film is silent on purpose, in seconds.
- Read every line aloud at speaking pace and time it.

## What the viewer has to be able to read

These were learned from a cold viewer who could not follow a film its maker found obvious.

- **Who people are to each other.** If it matters that she is the aunt and not the mother, something on screen or in a line has to say so. Viewers assume the nearest relation.
- **Counts.** If the story depends on a number (three in a row), every step of the count must be visible, in the same framing each time, so the viewer learns the tally. A count carried only by sound or by a face reacting is not a count.
- **Invented devices.** An object the film makes up as a marker has to be introduced clearly and look like nothing else in the frame. Small pale round things next to other small round things will be read as more of the same.
- **Results.** When the outcome of an action decides the story (which way something landed), it must be readable by shape and colour at film size.
- **Loose ends.** A deadline, a document, a threat that is set up has to be seen being resolved.
- **Who is in a wide or backlit shot.** A silhouette can be anyone. Let the person turn, or cut to them first.
- **Language.** If the lines are in a language part of the audience does not follow, subtitle them, and ask the user.

## The shot list

One row per shot: start and end, where the camera is and what lens, what the viewer must understand, what the subject does (a camera move is not an action), what is heard, spoken lines with their start times. Keep it in one machine-readable file that also generates the human-readable table, so the two never drift apart.

- Shot lengths vary. Long holds for the moments the film rests on, short cuts around them.
- Adjacent shots differ in size or angle.
- Mark which shots have a locked camera; they are candidates for painted backdrops (`building.md`).
- Mark the bar shot.

## The storyboard test (stage 3)

Before any real modelling:

1. Make one rough still per shot. Blockout quality is fine here, and say that it is a layout test.
2. Put them on one sheet in order, with the spoken lines as a transcript.
3. Give only the sheet and the transcript to a fresh reader who knows nothing about the film (a separate agent with no access to this conversation or the script). Ask: what happened, who are these people, what did each want, what was unclear, and whatever specific things the story depends on (can you count the yes answers; who is at the door at the end).
4. Their report goes to the user as it is. Every point where they were lost is fixed in the script or the shot list now, when it costs a few lines.
5. Run the test again on the finished cut. Small frames hide detail, so check any "could not read it" point yourself at full size before changing the film, and tell the user which points were the frame size and which were real.

A reader who knows the script cannot do this test. You cannot do it yourself.
